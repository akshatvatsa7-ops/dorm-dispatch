Dispatching the dorm maintenance crew

An optimisation model for the IIMA Estate Office, built on the real campus map and run on synthetic complaint data.

The Estate Office gets maintenance complaints all day and works them roughly in the order they arrive. That wastes a lot of walking, and it means an urgent complaint can sit behind twenty trivial ones. This project plans the day instead.

41% → 86% urgent complaints closed on time

Why the campus makes this hard

A main road cuts the campus in two. The Estate Office sits on the old campus; 19 of the 29 places that raise complaints are dormitories on the new campus. There is no crossing at road level, so every trip between the two halves goes through the walkway tunnel and takes about 11.3 minutes, against 2.1 minutes for a trip that stays on one side. Every technician starts the day on the wrong side of the road.

What the model decides

Given the complaints waiting at the start of a day, it decides which technician takes which jobs and in what order. A technician can only be given work in their own trade, every job has a window during which somebody is there to open the door, and everyone has to be back at the depot by the end of the shift. What it minimises is weighted lateness, with a small charge for walking so that walking breaks ties rather than driving the answer:

minimise   sum of (weight x hours late)  +  0.05 x walking minutes

Urgent complaints carry a weight of 10, normal ones 4, minor ones 1. Anything not done today stays in the queue and shows up tomorrow, so falling behind compounds.

Four ways to plan the day

Each method was built to show up what the previous one was missing. First come, first served is the baseline. Clarke and Wright's savings heuristic starts with one trip per job and merges the pairs that save the most walking, which on this campus naturally bundles new-campus work into a single trip through the tunnel. Priority insertion adds jobs one at a time, picking whichever gives the most urgency per extra minute of walking. The last method takes the savings plan and improves it with 2-opt, which untangles crossings, and Or-opt, which moves short runs of jobs around and, crucially, between technicians.

Is it actually any good?

For a single technician with up to 10 jobs we can find the true optimum by branch and bound and check. Savings plus improvement matched the optimum on every instance. Starting the search from the heuristic's answer also cut the number of nodes explored by more than half, which is the practical reason heuristics matter inside exact methods.

Six is not enough and the backlog runs away. Nine is enough. Twelve buys almost nothing. The last row was a surprise we kept in: carpentry looks like the idle trade, so we tried moving a carpenter's post to the busier trades at the same headcount, and it was far worse. With one carpenter that trade becomes a bottleneck and never recovers. The idle time was the buffer.

The most useful finding

Of the 282 urgent complaints raised over the month, 40 could never have met the four-hour promise, no matter who was sent: the room can only be accessed in the afternoon and the repair runs past the deadline. The optimised plan closed 41 urgent complaints late, of which 40 were of exactly that kind. That leaves 1 genuinely avoidable late urgent complaint in thirty days. We are within one ticket of the best any dispatcher could do, and what remains is a problem with the promise rather than with the plan.

What we would tell the Estate Office

  • Plan the day in the morning instead of working the queue as it arrives. Worth about a third of the crew's walking and roughly double the share of urgent complaints closed on time, with no extra staff.
  • Bundle by campus. Cross the tunnel, work the cluster, come back.
  • Do not hire. Nine technicians is enough for this load.
  • Do not rebalance the trades to match average workload. The quiet trade is quiet on average, not on a bad day.
  • Change the promise or change the access for complaints that can only be reached in the afternoon. No amount of scheduling fixes those.

Running it yourself

Plain Python, no libraries to install. The whole study takes under a minute.

git clone <this repository>
cd dorm-dispatch
python3 run.py

That rebuilds the walking network from the cached map data, runs every experiment, redraws the figures, writes the Excel workbook and regenerates every number quoted in the report. All the assumptions live in one file, src/instance.py, so anyone with the Estate Office's real figures can put them in and rerun.
